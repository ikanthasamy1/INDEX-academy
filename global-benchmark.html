How a Global Equity Benchmark Is Constructed

A practical guide to turning the world’s listed companies into an investable global equity universe.

1

Start with the Objective

Every global equity benchmark begins with a clear objective. At a high level, that objective might be:

“Represent the investable opportunity set available to institutional investors across eligible global equity markets.”

That single sentence drives a long series of decisions about which countries, securities and companies qualify, how large they need to be, and how they should ultimately be weighted.

2

Define the Geographic Universe

Providers generally classify markets according to accessibility, market infrastructure, regulatory environment, economic development and other investability characteristics. A broad global benchmark may combine developed and emerging markets, while some broader universes may also include frontier markets.

3

Define the Security Universe

Once eligible markets are identified, the provider defines which listed instruments can enter the benchmark. Not every exchange-traded security is treated as eligible equity.

Ordinary Shares→Eligible Equity-like Securities→Exclude Ineligible Structures→Candidate Universe

Exact treatment varies by methodology, but consistency is essential: the universe must be rules-based and reproducible.

4

Measure Company Size

The starting point is usually full market capitalisation:

Full Market Capitalisation = Share Price × Shares Outstanding

A company with 100 million shares outstanding at $20 per share has a full market capitalisation of $2 billion. But that figure does not yet tell us how much stock is realistically available to investors.

5

Adjust for Free Float

Free float reflects the proportion of shares considered available to public investors. Strategic stakes, controlling shareholders and other non-investable ownership may reduce the amount of a company that should be represented in a benchmark.

Investable Market Cap = Full Market Cap × Investability Factor

7

Create a Global Size Framework

Global benchmarks need a consistent way to distinguish large, mid and small companies across markets of very different sizes. Rather than relying on one arbitrary company-size cut-off, providers typically use systematic segmentation rules designed to maintain comparability across countries.

Large Cap+Mid Cap+Small Cap→Broad Market Universe
8

The balancing act

Representativeness — capture the intended market.

Investability — keep the benchmark replicable and practical.

Turnover control — avoid unnecessary constituent churn.

9

Assign Companies to Countries

Multinational companies can be difficult to classify. Incorporation, headquarters, primary listing, trading activity and operational exposure may point to different countries.

Incorporation+Headquarters+Primary Listing+Other Methodology Rules

The objective is not to find a philosophically perfect nationality, but to apply a consistent methodology to a complex real-world company.

11

Calculate Index Weights

For a free-float market-cap-weighted benchmark, constituent weights are determined mechanically from relative investable market size.

Index Weight = Constituent Investable Market Cap ÷ Total Index Investable Market Cap
Example weights
100%
Company A — 50%Company B — 30%Company C — 15%Company D — 5%
Key idea

The provider is not subjectively deciding that Company A “deserves” a 50% weight.

The weight is the result of the methodology and the company’s relative investable market capitalisation.

12

Combine Markets into a Global Benchmark

Once eligible countries are constructed consistently, their investable equity universes can be aggregated. Larger investable equity markets naturally contribute more weight than smaller ones.

Market A

Large investable market → high global representation.

Market B

Medium investable market → moderate representation.

Market C

Smaller investable market → smaller representation.

13

Calculate the Index Level

Index Level = Adjusted Market Capitalisation ÷ Divisor

The divisor maintains continuity when the structure of an index changes. Certain constituent changes and corporate actions can alter the index’s capitalisation without representing genuine investment performance. Divisor adjustments can prevent those mechanical changes from creating artificial returns.

14

Maintain the Benchmark

A global benchmark is a living rules-based system. Companies issue shares, buy back stock, merge, spin off businesses, change free float, conduct IPOs, become acquired or disappear entirely.

Scheduled Reviews

Reassess eligibility, size segmentation, float, liquidity and market coverage at predetermined review points.

Event-Driven Maintenance

Handle acquisitions, delistings, corporate actions and other events that cannot reasonably wait for the next scheduled review.

15

Why Similar Global Benchmarks Can Differ

Two providers can both seek to represent the global equity market and still produce different portfolios. Small methodological differences compound across thousands of securities.

Eligibility

Country classification, security types, minimum size and listing rules.

Investability

Free float, liquidity, foreign ownership and accessibility rules.

Maintenance

Review frequency, IPO treatment, buffers and corporate-action rules.

The big picture

A global equity benchmark is much more than a list of large companies. It is a systematic attempt to answer a deceptively difficult question:

What does the investable global equity market look like right now?
